Prescribed gravity versus self-consistent gravity

The user confirms that doubling resolution helps. This test asks why five-six cells per wave can still be insufficient for coupled gravity, and whether replacing numerical gravity by a recorded accurate potential removes the error. All previous numerical outputs remain untouched.

Experiment and interpretation

Every branch starts from identical point samples of the high-resolution reference wave and uses the same uniform wave grid and timestep. The spectral reference supplies its time-dependent potential at every kick endpoint. It is not frozen in time. The reference uses fourth-order splitting with two internal substeps, and independent finer-space and smaller-step references are checked.

Accurate potential replay uses the fine reference potential evaluated at target nodes. Coarse spectral replay instead computes a spectral potential from the reference density sampled on the target grid: its difference from accurate replay detects source sampling and coarse representation effects without feedback. FD2/FD4/hierarchy replay applies the approximate gravity map to that same reference density. Self-consistent runs recompute the selected potential from their own evolving wave. All replay runs are linear equations with a prescribed time-dependent potential. Approximate replay has no density-error feedback, but still has gravity approximation error.

Prescribed and self-consistent branches are not an additive error decomposition. Their errors can cancel, and self-consistency can restore an equilibrium. A ratio of final errors is not a universal feedback amplification factor. Global-FFT kinetics controls splitting and grid-representation error. Actual-table FFT and MATMUL Gram-FE are both included. The model excludes GAMER's flux correction, density rescaling, wave AMR, asynchronous ghost updates, and cosmological expansion.

Standing wave: gravity error exists without feedback

At six cells per carrier, FFT Gram-FE density errors are 0.000137142 with accurate replay and 0.000144927 with self-consistent spectral gravity. FD2 replay already gives 0.00434603, essentially the self-consistent FD2 error 0.00435009. The approximate gravitational forcing, rather than strong feedback amplification, accounts for most of the difference in this example. At all sampled reference times, the wave power shorter than six cells is below 8.031e-09.

Assessment of the difficult case and feedback

At N=96, accurate potential replay reduces FFT Gram-FE's strong-envelope density error from 4.62% (self-consistent) to 1.36%. Global FFT propagation with the same replay reaches 0.111%, so the remaining error is not all due to self-consistent gravity. However, as much as 37.6% of reference wave power lies below five cells per wavelength during this run, and unresolved density fluctuations reach 24.5% RMS at an intermediate time. The previous final-only bandwidth diagnostic understated this transient excursion. This is a propagation/representation failure in an evolving under-resolved regime, not evidence against the paper's six-cell free-wave convergence.

At N=192, accurate replay reaches 0.000691% density error, while self-consistent spectral gravity gives 0.0113%. Feedback can still matter when direct propagation errors are small; it is not an error source attributable solely to an inaccurate Poisson stencil. The coarse spectral replay control differs little from fine-potential replay in these final density metrics. This does not prove that all source aliasing is harmless.

For the localized core at N=64, FD2 replay has 0.268% density error and self-consistent FD2 has 0.200%. Self-consistency slightly reduces the final density error here. Unaligned wave errors of order 8-9% largely reflect an accumulated global phase: aligned wave errors are only about 0.28% and 0.19%. A large unaligned wave error must not be equated with a large halo-profile error.

Mass-conservation caveat and additional control

Strong-envelope FFT Gram-FE loses about 0.57% of total mass at N=96. The kinetic reconstruction does not include GAMER's separate conservation treatment. As a diagnostic only, globally normalizing the wave after every kinetic step reduces the self-consistent spectral-gravity density error from 4.62% to 3.19%, while prescribed-potential error changes from 1.36% to 1.34%. Mass drift contributes but is not the whole effect in this toy. Global normalization preserves no local flux and is neither a reconstruction of GAMER's correction nor a proposed production remedy.

Gravity refinement boundary in the core test

The core-only hierarchy uses a parent of spacing 2h, a centered child covering half the box, quadratic potential interpolation and a direct FD2 child solve. All fine wave data remain available. Root sources are point samples, not GAMER's cell averages. The potential is assembled from the parent outside the child and the child inside. One-dimensional harmonic boundary errors are affine; this test cannot reproduce the trace-free tidal errors possible in 2D/3D. It diagnoses a hierarchy mechanism, not the quantitative error of GAMER's six-level halo calculation.

Negative result: this centered 1D hierarchy adds almost no density error beyond uniform FD2 for the core. Self-consistent errors at N=64 are 0.19983% (hierarchy) and 0.19992% (uniform FD2). We have not reproduced the user's suspected AMR boundary failure in this experiment. The earlier 2D boundary tests are more informative about tidal communication.

It explains why high-order free wave convergence does not guarantee equally accurate self-gravitating dynamics.

The independent temporal test uses a plane wave and the commuting potential Phi(t)=sin(t), for which endpoint quadrature and the exact solution are known. It checks the replay time indexing and second-order temporal error. A manufactured source checks the direct child solve. Kinetic energy changes are accounted for at every substep; the sum closes. Halving dt changes both splitting error and the number of Gram-FE filter applications, so monotonic improvement is not assumed for that method. In particular, the strong-envelope prescribed density error increases from 1.36% to 1.47%, and self-consistent error from 4.62% to 5.43%. The qualitative attribution survives this control, but these numbers are not claimed to be a time-converged Gram-FE limit.

These experiments do not identify the dominant error in a cosmological halo, establish a universal cells-per-wavelength threshold, or show that a radial halo profile tracks pointwise density error. They do distinguish prescribed-force error, source-grid representation, self-consistent response, and wave propagation in cheap controlled cases.
