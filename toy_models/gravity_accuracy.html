Does better gravity reduce the required wave resolution?

Correction to the research premise: the user confirms that doubling resolution has always helped. The question is why faithful halo reconstruction needs more than the initially expected five–six cells per wavelength. Earlier statements treating failure under resolution doubling as an observed fact were a misunderstanding. This report supersedes that interpretation; previous numerical outputs remain preserved.

Result: better gravity can replace a factor-two refinement in selected coupled tests, and removes a measurable equilibrium-profile bias. It does not universally make five–six cells sufficient. Density bandwidth, wave propagation and gravity transfer across refinement boundaries can each become the next limitation. These are small controlled systems, not a cosmological halo validation.

1. Coupled interference: hold the wave evolution fixed

Dimensionless periodic 1D SP uses L=2π, iψₜ=−ψₓₓ/2+Φψ, Φₓₓ=g(|ψ|²−mean|ψ|²). The standing case superposes carriers ±16; the enveloped case adds a smooth amplitude envelope and a smaller carrier at 7. The same initial data, final time T=0.5 and 2,500 Strang steps are used for all compared gravity operators. Kinetic controls are global FFT and the previously reconstructed actual-table FFT/MATMUL Gram-FE maps. No AMR, mass correction, or cosmological expansion is present.

FD2, FD4 and spectral Poisson are all solved by FFT on uniform grids, so this comparison measures the discrete Laplacian rather than relaxation tolerance or solver speed. With s=sin²(kh/2), their positive Laplacian symbols are 4s/h², 4s(1+s/3)/h² and k². Dense reference operations and uniform spectral gravity are diagnostic oracles, not proposed production zoom-in algorithms.

It shows why a fixed initial points-per-wavelength number is insufficient. The standing-wave result also contains tiny generated sidebands; it establishes a final-time accuracy improvement in this test, not an all-times error bound.

3. Does gravity shift the equilibrium profile?

A periodic 1D self-gravitating core with mass 4 and box length 20 is solved to a nonlinear residual below 10⁻⁹. The kinetic operator is spectral for every case; only Poisson changes. The discrete stationary solutions are compared to a separately refined continuum-reference core. This tests a systematic bias without needing an instability or long time integration.

At N=64, FD2 makes the core about 0.476% denser at its peak and 0.403% narrower in RMS radius. FD4 reduces those biases to 0.0266% and 0.0187%. The FD4 radius bias at 64 cells is smaller than the FD2 bias at 128 cells. A stationary real core has no unique carrier wavelength, so these cell counts must not be translated into a de Broglie points-per-wavelength claim.

Starting every method from the same continuum core produces breathing around the altered equilibrium. A second case adds a weak phase perturbation and a small coherent incoming packet. At T=12, the perturbed-core density error is 0.00844% for N=64 FD4 versus 0.0518% for N=128 FD2. The temporal control changes these errors little. Radius oscillations have periods near three in these units, but the period estimates come from only a few peaks and a 0.03 sampling interval; the measured profile bias is firmer evidence than small apparent frequency differences.

The refined reference gravity transfers 25.3536 units of kinetic energy. Global Fourier kinetic steps conserve kinetic energy individually. Gram-FE can add or remove kinetic energy in the difficult coarse case; gravity improvements cannot repair that independent contribution. The split work budget closes within 10⁻⁹. The sampled integrated_gravity_work diagnostic in the general evolution JSON is under-resolved for rapid interference oscillations and is not used for conclusions; work.json contains the validated substep budget.

Reference checks include N=768 versus 1536 and halved time steps for 1D interference; independently solved N=257/385 stationary cores; N=512/1024 and halved time steps for core dynamics; N=192²/384² and halved steps for 2D evolution. The largest 1D interference reference time discrepancy is below 2×10⁻⁹. The 2D reference time discrepancy is 6.1×10⁻⁷, much smaller than the hierarchy errors. Actual comparisons also include timestep halving at coarse resolutions. For Gram-FE, halving the time step changes the number of per-step filters, so it need not monotonically improve spatial errors.

Reference density is evaluated at coarse sample locations using all reference Fourier coefficients, rather than low-pass resampling. A provisional version used the latter and was corrected before this report; all affected results were rerun. An independent analytic oscillatory-density identity checks the sampling. A quartic manufactured potential verifies FD4 boundary handling. No full GAMER binary was compiled or run.

Interpretation

A more accurate gravity treatment is justified as a concrete next improvement, not as a complete explanation or universal cure. There is now coupled evidence of a factor-two resolution saving in a weak interference case, stronger saving with spectral-reference gravity in a standing-wave case, and a reduced equilibrium bias. There is also a retained counterexample where newly generated bandwidth and the wave propagator dominate.

The simplest next production experiment would retain Gram-FE, improve Poisson order, and make the source and potential transfer consistent with that accuracy. Judge it by density-profile convergence and by the spectra of both ψ and |ψ|² throughout the evolution. Increasing only relaxation iterations will not correct the errors isolated here. The present evidence does not justify replacing the complete solver with a neural operator or assuming that any particular number of cells per wavelength will work universally.
